Specification Summary (formerly Convergent Core Analysis): Reading a Multiverse

By Moonlit Social Labs · April 3, 2026 · 8 min read

You've collected your data. You've cleaned it. Now it's time to analyze. But which analysis do you run? Do you winsorize outliers or exclude them? Mean-impute missing values or use listwise deletion? Run a parametric test or its non-parametric equivalent?

Each choice is defensible. Each gives a slightly different result. Gelman and Loken called this the garden of forking paths.

The Problem: One Dataset, 200 Results

Steegen and colleagues (2016) proposed multiverse analysis: run every reasonable specification and show the full distribution of results. Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson (2020) proposed the specification curve, which plots every estimate in order and adds a formal permutation test. Young and Holsteen (2017) proposed summaries of the same idea: how often the sign holds, how often the result is significant, and which modelling choices matter most.

Once you have run 200 specifications, you still need to describe them in a few sentences. Specification Summary does that bookkeeping for you. It is not a new method. It packages the summaries above in one place.

What Specification Summary Reports

In plain language

  1. Sign agreement. What share of specifications are positive, and what share negative? Does the more common sign reach your threshold (default 95%)?
  2. Spread. The median estimate, the interquartile range, the full range, and a trimmed mean that drops the top and bottom 5%.
  3. Significance. The share of specifications that are significant overall, after trimming, and significant in the dominant direction.
  4. Which choices matter. If you label each specification's decisions (for example outliers=IQR), it shows the median estimate at each level of each decision, how far apart those medians are, and which levels reverse the dominant sign.
  5. Different thresholds. The same checks at 90%, 95% and 99%.

Technical details

The input is a list of specification estimates, each with a p-value and optional decision labels. The tool computes:

  • Sign agreement: the share of estimates with the dominant sign, compared with a user-set threshold (default 0.95), and repeated at 0.90 / 0.95 / 0.99.
  • Spread: median, IQR, range, and the mean after trimming 5% from each tail.
  • Significance rate: share with p < α overall, after trimming, and in the dominant direction.
  • Decision influence: for each labelled decision, the median estimate at each level, the spread of those medians, and any level whose median has the opposite sign. This mirrors the model-influence idea in Young and Holsteen (2017).
  • Size words (small, medium, large; Cohen, 1988) appear only if you say the estimates are r or d.
What this can't tell you

Specification Summary is descriptive, not inferential. Every specification reuses the same data, so “95% of specifications agree” is not 200 independent confirmations. Agreement can be high simply because the specifications are similar to each other. For a formal test across specifications, use the specification-curve permutation test of Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson (2020).

When Should You Use It?

  • You have run, or plan to run, a multiverse or specification-curve analysis.
  • You want a short, transparent description of that output for a paper or presentation.
  • Reviewers have asked which analytic choices drive your result.

Example

Suppose you're studying whether a mindfulness intervention reduces anxiety. Your multiverse has 180 specifications varying by outlier handling, missing-data strategy, anxiety measure, covariate set, and parametric vs non-parametric test. The estimates are Cohen's d.

Specification Summary might report:

  • Sign agreement: 96.7% of specifications are negative, which passes the 95% threshold but not 99%.
  • Spread: median d = −0.41, IQR [−0.52, −0.31], trimmed mean −0.40.
  • Which choices matter: using the trait measure rather than the state measure moves the median from −0.45 to −0.22. No decision level reverses the sign.

That is a fuller description than “p = .03 with our preferred specification.” It is still a description of one dataset.
