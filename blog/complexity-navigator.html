Complexity Ladder (formerly Complexity Navigator): Comparing Simple and Complex Models

By Moonlit Social Labs · April 3, 2026 · 7 min read

A straight line is easy to explain. A curve may fit your data better. A very wiggly curve will fit your sample almost perfectly and predict new data badly. Choosing how complex a model should be is one of the oldest problems in statistics, and there are well-known tools for it.

Complexity Ladder runs those standard tools on a series of nested models and shows the results side by side. It is not a new method.

What It Fits

The ladder has these rungs:

  1. Intercept only (no predictor).
  2. Linear.
  3. Polynomial of degree 2, 3, 4 and 5. With several predictors, the higher rungs add pairwise products of predictors.

What It Reports

For every rung:

  • AIC (Akaike, 1974) and AICc, its small-sample correction (Hurvich & Tsai, 1989).
  • BIC (Schwarz, 1978), which penalises extra parameters more heavily as the sample grows.
  • Adjusted R².
  • 5-fold cross-validated RMSE, using the same folds for every model so the comparison is fair.

It then points out:

  • The model with the lowest BIC (highlighted).
  • The simplest model within ΔBIC < 2 of the best. Kass and Raftery (1995) describe BIC differences under 2 as “not worth more than a bare mention,” so a simpler model in that range is a reasonable choice.
  • The model with the lowest cross-validation error.
  • The first rung where cross-validation error goes up.
  • The first rung where adjusted R² improves by less than 0.01.

These criteria often agree. When they don't, that is useful to know: it means the data do not clearly favour one level of complexity.

What this can't tell you

This is ordinary model selection, and it has ordinary limits. Polynomials behave badly outside the range of the data, so a degree-4 fit can give absurd predictions just past your largest observation. In small samples the chosen degree is unstable: a few different cases could change the answer. The criteria pick among the models on the ladder; they cannot tell you whether a different kind of model (a spline, a threshold, a log transform) would be better, or whether the relationship is causal.

When Should You Use It?

  • You suspect a non-linear relationship and want to check whether a curve is worth the extra parameters.
  • A reviewer asks why you used a linear (or quadratic) term.
  • You want to show that your conclusion does not depend on one choice of model complexity.

Try It in PsyStat Lab

References

  • Akaike, H. (1974). A new look at the statistical model identification. IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control.
  • Hurvich, C. M., & Tsai, C.-L. (1989). Regression and time series model selection in small samples. Biometrika.
  • Kass, R. E., & Raftery, A. E. (1995). Bayes factors. Journal of the American Statistical Association.
  • Schwarz, G. (1978). Estimating the dimension of a model. The Annals of Statistics.
